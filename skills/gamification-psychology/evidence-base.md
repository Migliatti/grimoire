# Evidence base

Claims an engagement-mechanic argument may rest on, what each study actually measured, and
where the popular version overstates it. Cite the study, not the deck that quoted it. When a
claim is not on this list, treat it as unsourced until researched.

## Rewards and motivation

**Tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation.** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *A
meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic
motivation*, Psychological Bulletin 125(6), 627-668. 128 experiments. Engagement-contingent,
completion-contingent and performance-contingent tangible rewards all significantly reduced
free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28 respectively). Verbal rewards and
positive feedback did not, and could raise it when experienced as informational rather than
controlling.
Design consequence: the same badge is a competence signal or a controlling incentive
depending on how it is framed and on whether it is contingent on continuing to be watched.
<https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf>

**Points, levels and leaderboards moved output, not motivation.** Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch &
Opwis (2017), *Towards understanding the effects of individual gamification elements on
intrinsic motivation and performance*, Computers in Human Behavior 71, 525-534. An experiment
isolating individual elements found gamification increased task completion but did not affect
intrinsic motivation or competence need satisfaction; the authors read the elements as having
acted as extrinsic incentives.
Use this as the honest ceiling claim: PBL reliably moves the counted behavior. That is not
the same as the user wanting to do it.
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563215301229>

**Gamification made a course worse over a semester.** Hanus & Fox (2015), *Assessing the
effects of gamification in the classroom: a longitudinal study on intrinsic motivation, social
comparison, satisfaction, effort, and academic performance*, Computers & Education. Two
courses, same curriculum, one with a leaderboard and badges, measured at four points across a
16-week semester. Students in the gamified course declined in motivation, satisfaction and
empowerment relative to the control, and the effect on final exam scores was mediated by
intrinsic motivation.
This is the strongest published counterexample to "gamification lifts engagement". It is one
study, in a classroom rather than an app — state both when citing it.
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000>

**"Does gamification work?" — it depends, and the literature says so.** Hamari, Koivisto &
Sarsa (2014), HICSS 47. Review of 24 empirical studies: most reported positive effects for
some motivational affordance, with results heavily dependent on context and on the users.
There is no context-free effect size for gamification, and any deck that supplies one is
inventing it. <https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/hicss.2014.377>

**Counterproductive effects in a shipped gamified product.** Diefenbach & Müssig (2019),
*Counterproductive effects of gamification: an analysis on the example of the gamified task
manager Habitica*, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 127. A qualitative study
followed by a two-week field study with 45 users. It documented users optimizing the game
layer rather than the underlying tasks, including procrastinating on real work in favour of
game maintenance.
What it does not establish: a general law that added features reduce engagement, or any
quantified curve. Cite it for the mechanism, never for a number.
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581918305135>

## Progress, closure and goals

**Goal-gradient and illusionary progress.** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), *The
goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected: purchase acceleration, illusionary goal progress, and
customer retention*, Journal of Marketing Research 43(1), 39-58. Café reward-card holders
bought coffee more frequently the closer they were to the free one, and an illusion of
progress accelerated goal completion and raised retention in the program.
<https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal-Gradient_Illusionary_Goal_Progress.pdf>

**Endowed progress.** Nunes & Drèze (2006), *The endowed progress effect: how artificial
advancement increases effort*, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4), 504-512. Field experiment
with 300 car-wash loyalty cards. A 10-stamp card pre-stamped twice and an 8-stamp card
required identical remaining effort; redemption was 34% versus 19%.
Design consequence: a bar that starts above zero outperforms an identical bar starting at
zero. This is also the mechanism behind manipulative progress displays — the honest use
requires the remaining effort to be stated truthfully.
<https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/500480>

**Completion drive: what actually survives.** Ghibellini & Meier (2025), *Interruption, recall
and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects*, Humanities and
Social Sciences Communications 12, article 962. Excluding Zeigarnik's own 1927 data, the
recall ratio of interrupted to completed tasks was 0.99 — interrupted tasks were about 49% of
items recalled, meaning no memory advantage. A general tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks
(the Ovsiankina effect) did hold. The authors conclude the Ovsiankina effect represents a
general tendency while the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity.
Design consequence, stated precisely: a visibly incomplete, closable unit invites resumption.
Do not justify a closing-ring design by claiming unfinished tasks are remembered better —
that is the half that failed to replicate. <https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w>

## Variable reward

**Intermittent reinforcement produces more persistent responding than continuous
reinforcement**, and unpredictability is the active ingredient: a reward that can be predicted
generates no prediction error. That is the defensible form of the claim.

The indefensible form is a neurochemical story about a named product. "Dopamine hit" is not
evidence for a design decision; it restates the design decision in biological vocabulary. If a
mechanic needs neuroscience to justify it, it has no behavioral evidence behind it.

**Randomized paid rewards are a regulated category, not a design pattern.** As of mid-2026:
Belgium treats paid loot boxes as gambling and they are effectively unavailable there; the
Netherlands' 2020 enforcement against randomized packs was overturned on appeal in 2022,
narrowing but not removing exposure; the UK has not classified them as gambling, but a 2023
industry code requires parental consent for under-18 purchases; the EU has no dedicated law in
force, the European Parliament's November 2025 resolution urged the Commission to outlaw loot
boxes in games accessible to minors, and the Digital Services Act already prohibits platform
practices exposing minors to compulsive spending; Brazil prohibits loot box sales to under-18s
from March 2026. Apple and Google both require published odds before purchase.
This area moves — verify the current position before advising rather than repeating this
paragraph as settled. <https://blog.promise.legal/lootbox-regulation-2026-game-studios/>

## Bounded competition

**Foursquare replaced a global ranking with a scoped one.** In May 2014 Foursquare froze
mayorships and badges and moved a redesigned mayorship into Swarm, where a user competes with
friends rather than the whole user base and a place can have several mayors. The reported
problem was that as the network grew a global crown became unwinnable for almost everyone, and
badges lost meaning as their number grew.
This is a product decision reported at the time, not a controlled experiment. Cite it as an
illustration of the failure mode, not as measured evidence.
<https://venturebeat.com/mobile/foursquares-new-swarm-app-means-the-death-of-badges-mayors>

**Strava scopes competition to a segment, and added a second axis.** Segment leaderboards rank
only athletes who actually rode or ran that stretch, so achievability scales with the user's
own context. Local Legends, described in Strava's engineering write-up, ranks *frequency* on a
segment rather than speed, creating a second track that effort can win where speed cannot.
<https://medium.com/strava-engineering/building-local-legends-290879265c83>

## Streaks: first-party evidence only

Duolingo's engineering blog reports A/B results for streak-adjacent features: a streak wager
produced statistically significant increases in Day-1, Day-7 and Day-14 retention, with Day-7
the largest at +14%; a weekend amulet made users 4% more likely to return a week later and 5%
less likely to lose their streak. The same post reports that learners who binged were more
likely to abandon the app than learners who paced themselves.
These are vendor-reported experiments on one product with one audience. They are the best
public numbers available and they are not a general law.
<https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/>

## Claims to refuse

- **"Users with a 7-day streak are 3.6x more likely to be retained."** A correlation
  circulated without its selection effect: users who reach day 7 were already the motivated
  ones. Never present a completion-conditioned multiple as causal lift.
- **"Gamification increases engagement by 48%"**, **"90% of employees are more productive with
  gamification"**, and the rest of the statistics-roundup genre. These circulate across vendor
  blogs with no traceable primary study. Do not repeat them, with or without a citation.
- **"It takes 21 days to form a habit."** From Maltz's *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960), an
  observation about plastic-surgery patients adjusting to their appearance — not habit
  research. Lally et al. (2010) found a median of about 66 days to reach automaticity, with a
  range of 18 to 254 days across participants and behaviors: a median with enormous spread,
  not a schedule.
  <https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally>
- **Any general percentage for how much streaks lift retention.** No such number exists across
  products. Duolingo's own figures above are the honest substitute, with the product and the
  method attached.
- **Any invented confidence rating.** Labelling a made-up range "moderate confidence,
  consistent across published A/B work" fabricates a literature, which is worse than the bare
  number. If a range is an estimate, call it an estimate and show the reasoning.
